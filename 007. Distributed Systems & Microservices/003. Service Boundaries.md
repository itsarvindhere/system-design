Service Boundaries means deciding what responsibility belongs inside one service and what responsibility should belong in another service.

In microservices, this is one of the most important design decisions.

Because once you split a system into services, the boundary you choose affects -

    1. Data ownership
    2. Communication between services
    3. Team ownership
    4. Deployment independence
    5. Scaling
    6. Reliability
    7. Complexity

So, service boundaries are not just about splitting the code. They are about splitting business responsibility.

A good service boundary usually groups together things that belong to the same business capability.

For example, in an e-commerce system, some natural capabilities are -

    1. User management
    2. Order management
    3. Payment processing
    4. Inventory management
    5. Notification delivery

So, one service may own users, another may own orders, another may own payments.

That is usually much better than splitting services in random technical ways. For example, this is usually a bad split -

    One service for controllers
    One service for validation
    One service for database access

That is not a business boundary.

That is only technical separation, and it usually creates too much coupling.

# WHY SERVICE BOUNDARIES MATTER?

If boundaries are good, the system becomes easier to understand and evolve.

If boundaries are bad, the system becomes painful very quickly. Bad service boundaries often create problems are -

    1. Too many cross service calls
    2. Confusing ownership
    3. Shared data problems
    4. Frequent coordination between teams
    5. Harder deployments
    6. Slow feature development
    7. Distributed monolith behavior

So, boundary design is one of the main factors that decides whether microservices help or hurt.

## HIGH COHESION AND LOW COUPLING

A good service boundary tries to achieve two things -

    1. High Cohesion
    2. Low Coupling

High Cohesion means things that strongly belong together stay inside one service.

Low Coupling means services should not depend too much on the internal details of other services.

For example, let's say order creation always needs order items, order status, order total, and order history.

These things likely belong together in the same service because they are closely related.

But, payment gateway integration may belong in another service because it is a separate business concern.

So -

    Keep closely related things together
    Separate things that change for different reasons

That second line is very important.

## CHANGE BOUNDARY

A good way to think about service boundaries is this -

    When tends to change together should often live together

For example, if discount rules, cart logic, and checkout validation always change together, splitting them too early into separate services may create lots of coordination pain.

One the other hand, if notification delivery changes independently from inventory logic, separating them makes more sense.

This is somethings called designing around change boundaries.

## OWNERSHIP BOUNDARY

A service boundary is sometimes also called an ownership boundary. That means one team should usually be able to run a service clearly.

For example -

    Payments team owns Payment Service
    Orders team owns Order Service
    Search team owns Search Service

If many teams constantly need to change the same service for unrelated work, that may indicate the boundary is too broad.

If one feature requires touching five tiny services all the time, the boundaries may be too fragmented.

So, team structure and service boundaries often influence each other.

## DATA OWNERSHIP

A very important rule in microservices is that each service should usually own its own data.

For example -

    Order Service owns order data
    Payment Service owns payment data
    Inventory Service owns stock data

Other services should not directly update that data. Instead, they should communicate through APIs or events.

This matters because shared database access across services often destroys boundary clarity.

If five services freely read and write the same tables, then the services are not really independent. That often leads to a distributed monolith.

# BAD BOUNDARIES

Here are come common examples of bad boundaries -

    1. Splitting by technical layers instead of business capability
    2. Creating too many tiny services
    3. Sharing one database across all services
    4. Having one service constantly call many others for basic work
    5. Unclear ownership of data or business rules
    6. Needing coordinated deployment for every small change

For example, if Order Service cannot do anything without directly calling User Service, Pricing Service, Discount Service, Tax Service, Cart Service, and Inventory Service in one synchronous chain, the design may be too fragmented.

That creates a lot of operational and development pain.

# DISTRIBUTED MONOLITH

A distributed monolith is a system that looks like microservices from the outside, but behaves like a tightly coupled monolith underneath. For example -

    Many services exist
    But they must be deployed together
    They share the same database
    They depend heavily on each other
    One small change breaks many services

This often happens because service boundaries were chosen badly.

So, one of the main goals of a good service boundary is to avoid creating distributed monoliths.

# HOW TO FIND GOOD BOUNDARIES?

There is no perfect formula, but some useful questions are -

    - What business capability does this service own?
    - What data should this service own?
    - What rules belong here?
    - Which parts change together?
    - Which parts need independent scaling?
    - Which team should own this?
    - Can this service work with limited knowledge of others?

These questions help find healthier boundaries.

A lot of service boundary design is influenced by domain thinking.

The idea is that the business domain has natural subdomains or bounded areas.

For example, in e-commerce, these may be -

    Catalog
    Search
    Cart
    Orders
    Payments
    Inventory
    Shipping

Each of these can become a possible service boundary if the system is large enough. This does not mean every subdomain must become a microservice immediately.

It just means the business domain gives clues for good boundaries.

# COARSE GRAINED VS FINE GRAINED BOUNDARIES

A coarse-grained boundary means a service owns a larger area of responsibility.

A fine-grained boundary means the responsibility is split into smaller services.

For example -

    Coarse-grained:
        Commerce Service

    Fine-grained:
        Cart Service
        Order Service
        Pricing Service
        Discount Service

Neither is always correct.

If boundaries are too coarse, services become large and harder to manage.

If boundaries are too fine, the system becomes chatty and over-fragmented.

So, boundary design is about balance.

A lot of teams make the mistake of going too fine-grained too early.

# START BROADER, SPLIT LATER

In many systems, it is better to start with broader boundaries and split later when there is a clear reason. For example -

    Start with Order Management Service
    Later split payments or shipping if scale or ownership needs justify it

This is often safer than aggressively splitting everything into tiny services from day one.

Because once services are separated, merging or changing boundaries can be painful. So, early over-splitting is a common mistake.

# BOUNDARIES CHANGE OVER TIME

This is also important. A service boundary is not always permanent. As the business grows, traffic changes, and teams expand, the boundaries may need to evolve.

For example -

    A large Order Service may later split into
        Checkout Service
        Order Service
        Fulfillment Service

This is normal.

Good architecture is not only about choosing boundaries once.

It is also about knowing when they should change.
