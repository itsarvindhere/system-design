Delivery semantics describe what guarantee a messaging system gives about message delivery between the producer and the consumer.

In distributed systems, messages can be delayed, duplicated, lost, or retried because networks fail, services crash, acknowledgements get lost, or consumers process slowly.

So, the important question is not just -

    "Was the message sent?"

The more important question is -

    "What can the system guarantee about the delivery?"

This is what the 'Delivery Semantics' mean.

# TYPES OF DELIVERY SEMANTICS

There are three common delivery semantics -

    1. At most once
    2. At least once
    3. Exactly once

## 1. AT MOST ONCE DELIVERY

At most once means a message is delivered zero times or one time. It will not be delivered more than once.

This sounds nice at first, but the downside is that the message can be lost.

So, the meaning is -

    The system avoids duplicates
    But it may lose messages.

For example -
    
    1. Producer sends message
    2. Broker forwards message
    3. Consumer crashes before processing the message or acknowledgement handling failed
    4. Message is gone
    5. No retry happens

So, the consumer may never see that message again.

That is why it is called At Most Once because the message is delivered no more than one time.

Use this only when occasional message loss is acceptable. Some examples include -

    1. Non-critical Analytics
    2. Metrics
    3. Logging
    4. Temporary Notifications
    5. Best-effort monitoring events

In these cases, losing a small number of messages may be acceptable.


## 2. AT LEAST ONCE DELIVERY

At least once means a message is delivered one or more times. The system tries hard not to lose the message.

If it is not sure whether the processing succeeded, it may send the message again. So, the meaning is -

    The system avoid message loss, but duplicates can happen.

For example -

    Producer sends a message
    Consumer receives it
    Consumer processes it
    Consumer crashes before acknowledgement reaches the broker
    Broker thinks processing failed
    Broker redelivers the message

Now, the same message may be processed twice.

This is very common in real systems. That is why at least once delivery usually required idempotent consumers.

For example, if the message means -

    SendWelcomeEmail

And the consumer processes it twice, the user may receive two emails.

So, the consumers need protection against duplicate processing. For example -

    Check if event_id already processed
    Ignore duplicate if already handled


Use at least once when losing messages is more dangerous than processing duplicates. For example -

    Payment events
    Order events
    Inventory updates
    Webhook processing
    Background jobs

In most real systems, at-least-once is the most common practical model.

## 3. EXACTLY ONCE DELIVERY

Exactly once means the message is processed exactly one time, with no loss and no duplicates. This is the ideal guarantee in theory. 

It sounds perfect, but in distributed systems, it is very difficult and expensive to achieve fully.

This is because producer, consumer, broker, network, and storage can all fail independently.

If the consumer processes the message but the acknowledgement is lost, the broker may retry. Now, the system must somehow know that the work really already happened or not. This is the hard part.

So, the exactly once delivery usually requires additional coordination, transactional support, deduplication logic, or carefully designed system boundaries.

In practice, many systems that say "exactly-once" actually mean something narrower, such as -

    Exactly-once within a specific system boundary
    Exactly-once publishing into the broker
    Exactly-once stream processing under certain guarantees

It does not always mean that every side effect in the whole real work happens exactly once.

For example, if a system writes to a database exactly once but calls an external email provider twice, the whole business outcome is not truly exactly-once.

So, exactly-once is not as simple as it sounds.

Use it only when the business case really requires that extra complexity.

# WHY DELIVERY SEMANTICS MATTER?

They matter because they directly affect system behavior. They influence -

    1. Data correctness
    2. Duplicate handling
    3. Retry strategy
    4. Consumer design
    5. Operational complexity

For example, if your queue gives at least once delivery but your consumer is not idempotent, you can create real business bugs like -

    1. User charged twice
    2. Email sent twice
    3. Inventory reduced twice
    4. Report generated twice

So, delivery semantics are not just theory. They affect the real design of the system.

The easiest way to think about them is -

    At-most-once:
        May lose messages
        No duplicates

    This means, prefer simplicity and accept loss.

    At-least-once:
        No intentional loss
        Duplicates possible

    This means, prefer reliability and accept duplicates.

    Exactly-once:
        No loss
        No duplicates
        Hard and expensive in practice

    This means, prefer correctness and high complexity.

In real systems -

    At-most-once is used for low-value events
    At-least-once is used for most important business events
    Exactly-once is used carefully and only where truly needed

This is why idempotency is such an important topic in asynchronous systems.

Many systems do not try to guarantee perfect exactly-once behavior across the whole world. Instead they do this -

    - Use at-least-once delivery
    - Make consumers idempotent
    - Add retries
    - Track processed message IDs
    - Design side effects carefully

This gives strong practical reliability without needing impossible guarantees everywhere.

For example, suppose an Order Service publishes -

    OrderCreated

Now, consider the three semantics.

At most once -

    The event may reach the Inventory Service once
    Or it may be lost
    But it will not be delivered again

At least once -

    The event will be retried until delivered
    But Inventory Service may receive it more than once

Exactly once -

    Inventory Service should process the event one time only
    No loss
    No duplicate effect
    This requires stronger coordination

Also, Delivery Semantics describe the guarantee of message delivery, not automatically the guarantee of business outcome.

For example, A broker may deliver a message exactly once. But, the consumer may still execute the database update twice due to bad logic.

OR

A message may be delivered at-least-once. But the consumer may still make the final business outcome effectively exactly-once by using idempotency.

So, message delivery guarantee and business correctness are related, but they are not exactly the same thing.
